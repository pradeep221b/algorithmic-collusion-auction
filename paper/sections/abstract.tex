Three independent tabular Q-learners bid repeatedly into a stylised uniform-price electricity auction: three symmetric 60~MW firms, marginal cost \eur{10}/MWh, inelastic demand of 100~MW. After 5M rounds every seed has converged, at a mean collusion index $\Dl$ of 0.621 (95\% CI 0.612--0.630). Forcing one firm to undercut for a single round lowers its rivals' bids in 91\% of seeds, but they return to their old level within 24 rounds in only 21\%, and the verdict for a given seed changes with the identity of the deviator in 61 of 100.

The standard indicators nevertheless fail to attribute the price to tacit collusion. Removing the future ($\gamma = 0$) leaves $\Dl$ at 0.370, above the collapse threshold of 0.25 fixed beforehand; removing memory raises it to 0.943. A profitable unplayed deviation exists in every seed, including every seed of a memory-0 control that can anticipate nothing.

Five further experiments, two of whose predictions failed, support a reading of the memory-0 price as the small-noise limit of an $\varepsilon$-greedy Edgeworth-type cycle: it survives ten times as many exploratory rounds, a restart of exploration at $\varepsilon = 0.5$, and a change to pay-as-bid. One of the five does separate the two learners. The share of temptations that a single Bellman backup would already flip is 100\% in every memory-0 seed, and at most 80\% in any full-learner seed against a mean of 31\%. It tells a decision resting on a learned continuation from one resting on a stale estimate; the behavioural indicators do not.
