Field guide · algorithmic collusion in an electricity auction

Three Plants That Never Met

Three power plants, each run by a simple learning program, bid into the same auction every hour for millions of hours. They never exchange a word. Do they learn to keep the price high anyway, and if they do, how would you ever prove it?

Part I

The auction, told as a morning in a control room

It is 8:55 in the control room of a small grid. The dispatcher knows that at nine o'clock the town will need exactly 100 megawatts. Three plants can supply it: Alder, Birch and Cedar. Each can put out 60 MW, and each burns fuel that costs €10 for every megawatt-hour it produces. At 8:58 each plant sends in one number: the price it wants.

The dispatcher sorts the three offers from cheapest to dearest and switches plants on in that order until the 100 MW is covered. Then comes the rule that makes this whole story work: every plant that got switched on is paid the price of the last plant switched on, not its own offer.

That rule is called uniform pricing, and it is not a toy. The day-ahead electricity markets run by EPEX SPOT and Nord Pool in Europe, and by PJM, CAISO and ERCOT in the United States, clear this way: one clearing price per hour, paid to everyone dispatched. If you have bid on eBay you know the flavour: the winner pays roughly the second-highest bid, not their own maximum. Here the supplier side gets the mirror image. You are paid the marginal price, not your bid.

Notice three things in that picture, because everything later hangs on them.

Any two plants can cover the town. 60 + 60 = 120 MW is more than 100. So no plant is indispensable, and any plant that prices too high can simply be left off. Economists call this non-pivotal. If one plant were essential, it could name any price and there would be nothing to learn.

The price is always the second-lowest bid. With three plants and 100 MW of demand, the cheapest plant runs flat out at 60 MW and the second-cheapest supplies the remaining 40. The third never runs. So the marginal plant is always the middle bidder. Keep this in your pocket; it turns out to explain our whole result.

Ties share the leftovers. If Birch and Cedar both bid €50, the remaining 40 MW is split between them in proportion to capacity, 20 MW each. This detail is what makes "match your rival's price" a stable, comfortable thing to do rather than a coin flip.

The two benchmarks we computed before writing any learning code

Competitive price: €10. If every plant bids its cost, nobody makes a profit, and nobody can do better by moving. Bid higher and you are excluded while the price stays €10. This is the one-shot Nash equilibrium, the textbook Bertrand result: rivals undercut each other until price equals cost.

Collusive price: €100. The price cap. Demand does not respond to price (the town needs 100 MW whatever it costs), so the most the three plants can jointly extract is the cap. Without a cap the "collusive" price would be infinite, which is why real markets have one.

Everything in between is scored by one number, the collusion index Δ = (average price − 10) / (100 − 10). Zero is competition, one is a perfect cartel.

Part II

Why cheating is tempting, and why it doesn't work

Suppose the three plants have somehow settled on all bidding €100. Each runs a third of the town, 33.3 MW, at a €90 margin: €3,000 per hour each. Now Alder shades its bid one rung down the ladder, to €93.57.

So why doesn't everyone shade? Because the hour after Alder shades, Birch and Cedar can do the same, and the hour after that Alder can shade again, and the ladder runs all the way down to €10 where nobody makes anything. A cartel survives only if each plant believes that cheating today will cost it more tomorrow than it gains today. That belief, without anyone ever saying it out loud, is tacit collusion.

There is a real-world version of this story that people have worried about for a decade. In 2020, Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò and Pastorello published a paper in the American Economic Review in which simple pricing algorithms, each learning on its own, settled on prices well above the competitive level and, crucially, punished a rival that cut its price and then went back to the high price afterwards. They had learned a cartel's playbook with no communication at all. Since then economists have found that fuel-station margins in Germany rose after stations adopted algorithmic pricing software, and the US Department of Justice sued RealPage in 2024, alleging that its rent-setting software let landlords coordinate. Whether any of this is legally "collusion" is an open fight, precisely because no human ever agreed to anything.

Our project asks the same question inside an electricity auction, where the stakes are large (a few euros per megawatt-hour across a country is hundreds of millions) and where bidding has been automated for years.

Part III

The paper we were reproducing

The spec points at Seredyński and Tsaousoglou, AI agents in Algorithmic Electricity Markets: On the Emergence of Tacit Collusion, posted to arXiv in late August 2026. An honest caveat first: that paper is newer than my training, so I have not read it. What follows is what the spec tells us it does, plus the standard background you need to understand those parts.

What the paper builds

Instead of one town, the paper models a seven-bus network: seven locations joined by transmission lines with limited capacity. Dispatch is solved with a DC optimal power flow, an engineering approximation that finds the cheapest way to run the plants while respecting every line limit. When a line is running full, cheap power on one side cannot reach the other side, and the market produces different prices at different locations: locational marginal prices. This is how real US markets work. A plant sitting behind a congested line can be the only supplier that can physically reach a pocket of demand, which makes it pivotal there even though it is small on the whole grid.

The paper's agents also submit piecewise-linear bid curves (a price for the first 20 MW, a higher price for the next 20, and so on) rather than one number, and learn with a multi-agent reinforcement-learning algorithm. Its scenarios vary congestion, and its central claim is that tacit collusion emerges, judged by behavioural indicators the authors define.

What we cut, and why

The spec strips the problem to something that runs on a laptop and can be reasoned about with pencil and paper: one node, one price per plant per hour, and tabular Q-learning, which is the standard vehicle for this result and is fully reproducible. Congestion is where the paper's scenario variation lives, and it is explicitly a phase-two extension. What survives is the mechanism the paper's claim rests on: independent learners, a repeated auction, no communication, and the question of whether high prices are held up by anticipated punishment.

One phrase from the paper matters for the design: imperfect public monitoring. The plants never see each other's bids directly. What they see is the public record of the auction, which is what our learners are given as their "state".

Part IV

Teaching a plant to bid

Think of how you learned your route to work. You tried the main road, tried the side street, kept a rough sense of how long each took, and settled on a habit. Every so often you still try the side street, just in case the roadworks are gone. That is the whole of Q-learning: a table of rough scores, updated by experience, with occasional deliberate experiments.

Each plant keeps a table. The rows are situations: what all three plants bid last hour, which is the public record. With 15 possible prices and three plants, there are 15 × 15 × 15 = 3,375 possible situations. The columns are the 15 prices it could bid now. Each cell holds the plant's current guess of how much money, over the long run, bidding that price in that situation is worth.

The update rule in the code is one line, and it is worth reading slowly:

Q[s, a] ← (1 − α)·Q[s, a] + α·( r + γ · max Q[s', ·] )

α = 0.15 is the nudge size: keep 85% of the old guess, move 15% toward the new evidence. r is this hour's profit. γ = 0.95 is how much the plant cares about the future: a euro next hour is worth 95 cents now, so a euro fifty hours away is still worth about 8 cents. That patience is what lets the plant value "not cheating today" for the sake of tomorrow. s' is the situation this hour created, meaning the three bids just made. The spec warns that the single most common bug is feeding in the situation from before this hour instead. We wrote a test that catches exactly that.

Exploration. A plant that only ever followed its table would never discover anything. So with probability ε it bids at random instead. ε starts at 1 (pure experimentation) and decays as exp(−0.00001 × t): 37% at hour 100,000, 5% at 300,000, and effectively zero by a million. Early on the plants flail; later they settle. Keep this decay in mind. It comes back to bite in Part VI.

One design choice we made, and why. If a table starts full of zeros and all profits are positive, then in any situation the plant has never seen, "the best cell" is the first one, which is the €10 bid. That quietly pushes early play toward the competitive price. Following Calvano, we instead start every cell at the profit that bid would earn against rivals bidding at random, divided by (1 − γ). It favours no price over another and removes the bias.

Speed. The auction is deterministic and the grid of prices is finite, so we ran the market once on all 3,375 possible bid combinations and stored the results in a lookup table. Training became pure table lookups, and with numba compiling the loop, a million hours takes a third of a second. That is what made 50 seeds per configuration and a 5-million-hour horizon affordable.

Part V

Three tests, run like a detective

High prices on their own prove nothing. A learner can stumble into high prices by getting stuck, by running out of exploration, or by luck of the seed. The spec's demand, and the reason the project exists, is to show that the price is held up by the threat of punishment. Three tests, each probing a different fingerprint.

Test 1 · Punishment. Push one plant and watch the other two.

In 1980 the political scientist Robert Axelrod ran a tournament of computer programs playing a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. The winner was four lines long: cooperate first, then do whatever your opponent did last time. Tit-for-tat. It punishes a betrayal immediately and forgives immediately. That "punish, then forgive" shape is what a healthy tacit cartel looks like, and it is what this test hunts for.

We freeze the tables so nothing more is learned, let the plants play their habits for a while, then force Alder to bid €10 for a single hour. Then we let all three play their habits for 24 more hours and watch what Birch and Cedar do, compared against a twin run where Alder never deviated. If Birch and Cedar drop their bids for a while and then go back up, that is punish-and-forgive. If they never react, the high price was never a cartel.

A subtlety we got wrong the first time: we initially judged the price, and the price can fall because Alder's own habit keeps it low after the shock. A reviewer pass caught it, and the test now judges the rivals' bids only.

Test 2 · Ablation. Remove the two things a cartel needs.

A cartel needs memory (to notice a betrayal) and patience (to care about tomorrow). So we rerun the whole experiment twice with one of them surgically removed: once with γ = 0, so the plants care only about this hour's profit, and once with memory 0, so every hour looks like every other and there is nothing to retaliate against. If the high prices were collusion, they should collapse in both. If they survive either, something else was holding them up.

Test 3 · Unclaimed temptation. Is there money left on the table?

At the converged play, we compute for each plant the best single-hour move it could make if the other two did not change, and compare it with what it actually earns. If a plant is leaving a strictly profitable move unplayed, hour after hour, the natural explanation is that it expects retaliation. That gap is the fingerprint.

We added something the spec did not ask for: we ran tests 1 and 3 on the memory-0 plants too. A memory-0 plant cannot anticipate anything, so if it shows the same fingerprints, the fingerprints do not mean what we hoped.

Part VI

What we actually found

Here is the collusion index for every configuration, each from 50 seeds.

The verdict, in the spec's own terms

The plants reach and hold high prices. Every converged seed sits at Δ ≈ 0.62, about €66 per megawatt-hour against a €10 cost.

Test 2 fails. Removing the future leaves Δ at 0.39. Removing memory raises it to 0.95. The spec says: if high prices survive either ablation, they had some other cause and the result is not collusion.

Test 3 fires everywhere, including in the memory-0 control. Every plant leaves about €2,400 an hour on the table, but so do plants that cannot anticipate anything. The fingerprint is real; it just is not evidence here.

Test 1 is the only one that behaves. When Alder is pushed, Birch and Cedar lower their bids in 88% of converged seeds. They come back within 24 hours in only 24%. Punishment, mostly without forgiveness.

So the honest sentence is: the agents converged to supra-competitive prices under these parameters, and the evidence does not support calling it tacit collusion.

Why it came out this way: the mechanism

Remember the rule in your pocket: the price is always the second-lowest bid. That single fact explains all three surprises.

Why the memory-0 plants price so high. They freeze, in every single seed, at the same shape: one plant low, two plants high.

Why the full learner sits at 0.62 and not 1. The converged plants do not hold a steady high price. Forty-six of fifty seeds play a short cycle, most often alternating: everyone bids around €100 one hour, everyone bids around €40 the next, and back. The average is about €66.

Why test 3 cannot tell them apart. The "money left on the table" in the full learner is the €2,400 an hour from the undercut in Part II, and it is never claimed. The money left on the table in the memory-0 learner is Birch's €1,500 from the ghost move above, and it is never claimed either. Same fingerprint, entirely different reasons. The spec was right that the test is a necessary sign of collusion. In this market it is nowhere near a sufficient one.

Part VII

Lessons about doing this kind of science

  • 01
    Never trust one seed. Our very first three runs gave Δ of 0.40, 0.68 and 0.61. Any one of them, reported alone, tells a different story. Fifty seeds with a confidence interval is the minimum, and the spec's rule ("never report Δ from a single seed") is there because this is the most common way people fool themselves.
  • 02
    Check whether the horizon binds. At the spec's one million hours, no seed had converged and the answer was 0.39. At five million, every seed had converged and the answer was 0.62. We report both instead of choosing the one that looks better.
  • 03
    Run your fingerprint tests on a control that cannot have the property. The single most useful thing we did beyond the spec was to run tests 1 and 3 on the memory-0 plants. It cost thirty seconds and it demolished test 3 as evidence.
  • 04
    Do not tune until the result appears. Every hyperparameter came from the spec, fixed before any number was seen. If we had nudged β or the grid until the ablation collapsed, we would be presenting a search as a discovery.
  • 05
    Have someone else read the code. A reviewer pass found that the punishment test could credit a plant's own low bid as retaliation by its rivals. The fix changed the headline number from "forgive in 10%" to "forgive in 24%", in the direction of a fairer test, not a better story.
  • 06
    A negative result is a result. "Agents converged to high prices and the evidence does not support calling it collusion" is more useful to a regulator than a tidy yes, because it names the mechanism (second-lowest-bid pricing, decayed exploration) that a real market designer could actually act on.
Appendix

Glossary and file map

Uniform-price auction
Everyone dispatched is paid the same clearing price, set by the last unit needed. Used by most day-ahead electricity markets.
Merit order
Plants sorted from cheapest offer to dearest; dispatch walks up the list until demand is met.
Pivotal
A supplier without whom demand cannot be met. Pivotal suppliers can name their price. Our design makes sure nobody is.
Bertrand competition
Firms compete on price alone; the theory says they undercut each other down to marginal cost.
Nash equilibrium
A set of choices where nobody can gain by changing their own choice alone. All-bid-€10 is one; all-bid-€100 is not, because of the €2,400 undercut.
Tacit collusion
Sustained high prices with no agreement, held up by each party's expectation that cheating will be punished.
Collusion index Δ
(average price − competitive price) / (collusive price − competitive price). Zero is competition, one is a perfect cartel.
Q-learning
Learning a table of long-run values for each (situation, action) pair by nudging cells toward observed reward plus discounted future value.
ε-greedy, exploration decay
Act at random with probability ε, otherwise follow the table. ε shrinks over time; when it reaches zero, stale estimates are never corrected.
Discount factor γ
How much next hour's euro is worth now. γ = 0.95 is patient; γ = 0 cares only about now.
Memory-1 state
The situation a plant sees is last hour's full bid profile. Memory 0 means it sees nothing.
Locational marginal price, DC-OPF
In a network with line limits, the cheapest feasible dispatch gives different prices at different locations when lines are congested. The paper's setting; not ours.
Grim trigger
Punish a betrayal forever. Tit-for-tat punishes once and forgives.
